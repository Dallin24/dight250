1890s–1910s

BEFORE THE RECORDS · NEW ORLEANS

A music made from many roots.

In New Orleans, Black communities shaped a new sound from African American musical traditions, blues, ragtime, spirituals, and brass-band practice. Creole musicians and the city's varied musical communities also helped make the early scene distinctive. Improvised lines, a strong shared pulse, and the push and pull between written parts and spontaneous invention were already central. There is no single birth date: jazz grew through people playing together.

1917

THE RECORDING INDUSTRY TAKES NOTICE

A recording travels farther than a bandstand.

The Original Dixieland Jass Band's 1917 Victor release is widely recognized as the first commercially issued jazz recording. The band was white; the recording industry's spotlight did not mark jazz's origin. Black New Orleans musicians had developed the music over years, and commercial access, publicity, and credit were unequal. The new medium nevertheless let listeners far from New Orleans encounter a version of jazz in their own homes.

1923–26

LOUIS ARMSTRONG CHANGES THE SPOTLIGHT

The solo steps out front.

In 1923, King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band recorded in Chicago with young cornetist Louis Armstrong. Armstrong's Hot Five sessions, beginning in 1925, made the expressive solo a thrilling focus: a player could take a tune apart, reshape its melody, and tell a personal story inside the ensemble. The records helped listeners hear the individual voice as a defining feature of jazz.

1935–39

SWING HITS THE AIRWAVES

When the whole band becomes a sensation.

On August 21, 1935, Benny Goodman's band played a celebrated set at Los Angeles's Palomar Ballroom. The crowd's enthusiastic response and the press that followed helped make swing a national youth craze. Big bands turned jazz into a mass dance music: arranged sections gave dancers a recognizable shape, while soloists made room for surprise. Black bandleaders including Duke Ellington and Count Basie were central innovators, even as segregation shaped who could perform, travel, and profit.

1940s

BEBOP · SMALL GROUP, BIG IDEAS

From dance floor to listening room.

In the early 1940s, musicians such as Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, and Mary Lou Williams developed bebop: quick tempos, intricate harmonies, and improvisations that demanded close listening. The style was not simply a rejection of swing; it was a new artistic language, built by Black musicians in clubs and late-night sessions. It changed how later audiences understood virtuosity and creative freedom.

1959–70

THE STUDIO OPENS NEW DOORS

Jazz changes its shape—and its sound.

In 1959, Miles Davis's Kind of Blue explored modal improvisation, while Dave Brubeck's Time Out made unusual meters part of a widely heard jazz album. In 1969 Miles recorded Bitches Brew, released in 1970, combining electric instruments, studio editing, and long grooves. Jazz-rock fusion reflected a changing musical landscape and brought jazz into closer conversation with rock, funk, and the album-oriented popular music industry.

1980s–2020s

HIP-HOP HEARS THE ARCHIVE

A record becomes raw material.

Hip-hop DJs and producers treated recorded sound as something to play, cut, loop, and rearrange. In 1991, A Tribe Called Quest's The Low End Theory brought jazz-informed bass lines and samples into a landmark hip-hop album; in 1993, Guru's Jazzmatazz, Vol. 1 brought jazz musicians into live collaboration with MCs. In 2015, Kendrick Lamar's To Pimp a Butterfly again put jazz improvisers and jazz-inflected arrangements into a major pop conversation. Robert Glasper's Black Radio III (2022) continued to bring jazz, R&B, and hip-hop artists into the same room.
