    November 2026

    ovember starts in style, with reviews of two inspiring new books on the Victorian period. Joti Bilkhu writes warmly about By Touch Alone: Blindness and Reading in Nineteenth-Century Culture, by Vanessa Warne, while Laurent Bury also finds much to enjoy in Chloe Ward's Art and Action: Painting for Social Change in Victorian England. It's fascinating to see, for example, how Braille script evolved, along with the printing techniques required to produce it; and how artists like Luke Fildes saw the potential of art to encourage empathy, and to improve both the individual viewer and the condition of life for the vulnerable.

    JB's work on the architect William Eden Nesfield progresses slowly, with a discussion of one of his masterpieces, Cloverley Hall (we already had a little about this) but news of a proposal to demolish part of a fine mixed-use Victorian terrace near Leicester Square sent her off course. Sir James Pennethorne, the town-planner in charge of the original "London Improvements," would have been aghast! It is certainly worth appreciating his vision for the area before any changes occur.

    So far this month our Contributing Editor from Canada, Philip Allingham, has been focusing on George Cruikshank's marvellously quirky illustrations for Grimms' fairy tales, such as this one of the Elves and the Shoe-maker, a favourite of publishers and commentators alike.

    We were delighted to hear from Philip Jackson again with some very useful source-material on Oscar Wilde — a round-up of contemporary reviews on Wilde's long poem, The Sphinx, with more reviews and critical commentaries of his work soon to follow.

    Thanks to Tim Willasey-Wilsey, we also have some new work on two important figures commemorated by marble busts in the University of London's Senate House library — Jeremy Bentham (by the French sculptor David d'Angers) and William Shaen (by the Pre-Raphaelite sculptor Thomas Woolner). Both sitters were closely involved in the founding of the University, and fully deserved to be honoured here. Shaen, in a coincidental link to Vanessa Warne's By Touch Alone, served on the board of management of the Royal Normal School for the Blind, and actively promoted higher education for the visually impaired.

    Correspondence: Once again, we have some very sad news to report — this time the recent death of Dr John Salmon, who took church photography to a professional level after retiring from his career in medicine, and contributed so many photographs of churches, stained glass (like the window on the right, designed by Walter Crane) and other ecclesiastical artwork to our site over the years. John was so generous and knowledgable, and has left a store of photographs that we can still draw on, as well as his informative books on the subject. He will be much missed, and not forgotten.

    Many thanks to Phillip Cheek, who noted an important omission in our series of commentaries on Sir Thomas Brock's well-known landmark outside Buckingham Place, the Victoria Memorial.


    October 2026

    he end of last month was an unusually busy time on the website. But before taking a look below, do check in first to see the new conferences and calls for papers with their various deadlines. Note, for example, that a symposium on the circulation of Victorian annuals, Christmas gift books, etc, is due to take place next month at Caen Normandie University. Keep an eye, too, on the list of books currently available for review maintained by our Managing Editor, Diane Josefowicz (and see also Diane's guidelines for reviewers). These are regular features, to which there are frequent additions.

    Our most recent book reviews are in two very different areas. One is an informative discussion by Alyssa Q. Johnson, of Lydia Murdoch's What We Mourn: Child Death and the Politics of Grief in Nineteenth-Century Britain. Murdoch, as Johnson explains, has looked at this kind of peculiarly painful bereavement from a wide perspective, showing how it came to be seen and acted on as an often avoidable tragedy, rather than simply inevitable — as outrage about present-day cases of hospital malpractice all too often reminds us. The other review this month is by Julia B. Juppenlatz, who looks at Women Staging and Restaging the Nineteenth Century, edited by Laura Monros-Gaspar and Victoria Puchal-Terol. The essays gathered here cover some exciting new territory, and the last one, on "the many works inspired by the Pre-Raphaelite artist, muse and poet Elizabeth Siddal," is likely to hold a particular appeal for many of our readers.

    Among much other new work, at the beginning of the month we finalised a substantial article from one of the Pugin Society's earlier publications — a very well-documented paper on "A.W.N. Pugin, Stained Glass, and the 1851 Medieval Court," by Jasmine Allen, Director and Curator of the Stained Glass Museum in Ely. This brings together a major figure, craft and event of the Victorian period: nothing conveys the various ups and downs of the whole project better than Pugin's own words, poured out in his eccentric orthography. Despite all his concerns, the Great Exhibition was, of course, a great success for the country as a whole, and for him personally. "It rains windows," as he said afterwards, in response to the increased demand for his work.

    Later in the reign, another highly original architect and designer had a more subtle influence. This was Edward Schroder Prior, and JB greatly enjoyed reviewing David Valinsky's The Architecture and Writings of E.S. Prior: Challenging the Arts and Crafts, with its detailed examination of his highly individualised buildings. Prior's contribution to the stained glass boom, incidentally, was the invention of "Prior's Glass," with its "flickering dancing light." Just a small part of his work, this was nevertheless significant: such experiments set him on the very threshold of a new age in architecture and design. Finding very little on our site about an earlier trend-setting country house architect, William Eden Nesfield, JB next provided an introduction to his work. More examples of this will follow soon, but news just in, about the demolition of Alverstone farmhouse on the royal estate at Osborne, proved distracting: it seemed well worth recording this example of Prince Albert's hands-on concern for the welfare of working folk, while the memory of it was still fresh.

    In another branch of the visual arts, a major contribution this month comes from our Senior Editor, Simon Cooke: his "Dalziels’ Bible Gallery: An Introduction and Guide" is part of his ongoing project for his next book,which will assemble a full account of the Dalziels' magnum opus. This work was on a truly grand scale, involving major artists of the age from G.F. Watts and Frederic Leighton to John Everett Millais. Surely nothing could better help to restore bible literacy today (a topic much in the news recently) than these powerful illustrations.

    Still in the illustrations section, our Contributing Editor from Canada, Philip Allingham, has now completed his work on Phiz's illustrations for William Harrison Ainsworth's utterly over-the-top sensational novel, Auriol; or, the Elixir of Life (1846), starting with a fateful interview in Hyde Park, and ending with a bit of an anti-climax (no spoilers!). This brings us to another piece of encouraging news: the BBC has contacted us for the use of Philip's recently completed work on another illustrator, Fred Barnard, in a documentary. Philip has also been very busy updating some of his numerous discussions of other illustrators, notably a series from 2012 on Edwin Austin Abbey. An example here is Abbey's take on the Peerybingles as they dote on the new addition to their family in Dickens's The Cricket on the Hearth. Improved scans and updated texts make a world of difference to this older work.

    Yet another project is also coming along well: our Contributing Editor from Poland, Andrzej Diniejko, has just added to his "New Woman Fiction" series with an account of Kathleen Caffyn (Iota)'s life and most successful and controversial novel Yellow Aster. Too frank for some readers, but welcome to many more, this raised important questions about what was truly needed for women's fulfilment. You may or may not agree with the heroine's findings!

    Dennis T. Lanigan's revision of our section on the artist Albert Joseph Moore is now complete, with discussions of many additional paintings, drawings and designs. These are eye-opening in their range — from his trademark paintings of women elegantly disposed among flowers or draped on couches (A Summer Night combines both elements), to such comparatively little-known and more lively drawings and designs as these tile designs made up by Minton.

    Tim Willasey-Wilsey's photographs of London monuments continue to inspire new accounts of famous figures — behind the equestrian statue of Robert Napier is a tale of immense abilities and fortitude, with a sad footnote involving an orphaned prince made famous by Julia Cameron's photograph of him. Tim also provided us with an early example of the Victorian drinking-fountain initiative, the "Boy with a Pitcher" in St James's Park, by Charles Mabey and Robert Jackson. From outside London, Tim sent in some excellent photographs of the historic and (remarkably) still fully functioning wooden Broomhill Bridge in the Scottish Highlands, and, later on, the little-noted late-Victorian war memorial to the Oxfordshire Light Brigade in Oxford. This led to a short piece about another memorial, this time in St Paul's, to Major A.B. Thruston, one of the regiment's officers, who was murdered in the Uganda Mutiny of 1897-98. It was sad to learn that Thruston's fate was so ill-deserved.

    Correspondence: We had some very sad news at the beginning of the month, when we heard that Shirley Nicholson, who contributed to our site over the last five years or so, not least by proof-reading, died quite suddenly but peacefully on 25 September. We send our heartfelt sympathy to her family, who kindly notified us, and remember with gratitude how generously Shirley shared with us her great store of knowledge about Sambourne House, Leighton House Museum, and the Kensington artists' circle. The material and connections that she brought us over recent years are a treasured legacy. But it is so hard to accept such a loss; she is greatly missed. The little picture of her shown here comes, by permission and with thanks, from a larger one accompanying Shirley's memorial notice on Leighton House and Sambourne House's Facebook page (2 October).

    More recent correspondence, of the more usual kind, has included some pleasant communications with Presidency University, Kolkata, where the alumni are researching the Victorian architect of their main building, William Henry White. We were delighted to learn of their interest. We do try to answer queries, or at least give some pointers to further resources. Note, though, that such resources can usually be found in the bibliographies at the end of individual entries — and giving opinions about the authenticity and value Victorian artwork (yes, we often get such requests) is entirely beyond our scope.


    September 2026

    eptember, and the shorter days are upon us. The first fruits of the month are some splendid additions to the "Horses in the Victorian Age" section, peer-reviewed under the direction of Kristen Guest (University of Northern British Columbia) and Ronja Charlotte Frank (Memorial University, St John's, Newfoundland), as part of the Equine Breed and the Making of Modern Identity project funded by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada. Topics range from British Cavalry Policy after the Anglo-Boer War, discussed by Chelsea Medlock, and women's equestrianism, discussed by Heidi Mikkelsen, to changes in women's riding habits, discussed by Jordyn Baratta. One intriguing item here was the divided "Hayes' Safety Skirt" for safer mounting: of course, it had to be kept closed when walking....

    As usual, there are new items on the conference listing, with one call for papers on the all too timely topic of "Planetary Precarity"; and a whole crop of new book reviews. JB looks at Julia Bolton Holloway's Elizabeth Barrett Browning and Her Circle: Laurel Garland, while our Managing Editor Diane Josefowicz reviews Sarah Bilston's The Lost Orchid: A Story of Victorian Plunder and Obsession. Appealing as they are in other ways, both books, like the "Planetary Precarity" topic, give rise to some troubling thoughts — the former because of its insinuations about Robert Browning, and the latter because it draws attention to the human and environmental cost of orchidomania. Even more disconcerting is the last part of a review by another of our editors, Jo Devereux: this fine scholarly assessment of Laura Fox Gill's John Milton in Nineteenth-Century Literature and Visual Culture ends with some ghoulish details which many of us would probably prefer not to have known!

    Coming in later in the month, Rebecca Nesvet's lively and dramatically titled "Release the Kraken! A Review of Richard Holmes's The Boundless Deep: Young Tennyson, Science, and the Crisis of Belief has its own disturbing resonance. Holmes is always eminently readable, and despite finding some scope for improvement, this review is overwhelmingly positive ("a wonderful, essential book"). Elizabeth Steere's review of Theatrical Afterlives: Nineteenth-Century Women's Novels on the Stage is equally enthusiastic about the way Marina Cano shows stage adaptations bringing fresh insights into a whole range of Victorian novels, from East Lynne to Cranford, exposing "concerns, sympathies, and values present but not overt in the source text." Particularly welcome here is the inclusion of amateur performances, which can often offer surprising perspectives on familiar texts.

    Turning to the visual arts, and Tim Willasey-Wilsey's new photographs and useful research leads, JB was able to write about one of Alfred Waterhouse's later works. This was the pioneering Lister Institute (now the Lister Hospital) on Chelsea Bridge Road, with its complex history and noble aims: it was the first medical research charity in the country. Tim also sent in a fine set of photographs of the Carabiniers (6th Dragoon Guards) Memorial nearby on Chelsea Embankment. As it happens, both have some relevance to the "Horses in the Victorian Age" project, since horses were used in medical research as well as warfare. There is no escaping the darker side of life in Victorian England.

    Meanwhile, in the illustrations section, our Senior Editor Simon Cooke has been busy working on his Dalziels' Bible Gallery project, with new essays on Thomas Dalziel, Edward Dalziel and E. F. Brewtnall. As for the Dalziel Brothers, we tend to discuss them together, but, from the illustrations selected here, it is clear that they amply repay individual attention. Disentangling the "Dalziel Brothers" not only from each other but from Edward's son, Edward G. (Gurden) Dalziel, has been tricky, to say the least, and Simon is now working on a longer account of the Bible Gallery which will bring all his new material together.

    The illustration section also sees new work by our Contributing Editor, Philip Allingham, on Arthur A. Dixon and his illustrations for the Collins Pocket Classics edition of The Tale of Two Cities, Little Dorrit, and others — this took time, partly because Philip made a detour to update Sol Eytinge's illustrations of the latter. He has also been working on Phiz's illustrations for Charles Lever's novel Barrington, a sequence which he first put online in 2002! Updating is essential if a long-established website like ours is to keep up with advances in technology and scholarship. Adding new material is only one part of the story.

    Still in the visual arts pages, we're happy to report that our hardworking art historian, Dennis T. Lanigan, is also updating older work as he adds to our section on Albert Joseph Moore — one of his favourite Victorian artists. We now have some of his choicest works in colour, rather than the original monochrome, and many new works, including the beautiful shimmering Sapphires. As usual, we're grateful to Scott Thomas Buckle for reading the commentaries and making helpful suggestions. Here again, there are many more works still to be included.

    Cheering us along in all this is Sylvia Hornsby's new discussion of the novelist George Meredith’s sense of humour. While his essay on the "Comic Spirit" became a classic of its time, his own deployment of "thoughtful laughter" tends to be neglected, especially in novels other than The Egoist, and Sylvia has selected some excellent instances of it in these other works, including One of Our Conquerors. The subject inspired JB to look up two Punch cartoons of the 1890s, making fun of Meredith's notoriously difficult style — examples of "thoughtful laughter" in the magazine's own inimitable style.

    We were delighted to receive some photographs from George Michaels of two family heirlooms shown on the TroveStar website: an exquisite presentation casket in silver and chiseled steel by Alexander Fisher, and a drawing for a stained glass window (St Paul Preaching at Athens), one of a series of celebrated windows in St Paul's, Morton, Lincolnshire, by Sir Edward Burne-Jones.

    Correspondence: Many thanks to Faith Stanley for informing us about a domain change for Liverpool's mercantile heritage site, so that we could update our links to it. Here's another kind of input that we greatly appreciate. Penelope Harris writes to inform us that her new book on the architect Joseph Hansom will be available from 1 October at a "more sensible" price (to be precise, £36.19 instead of the originally planned £50.00) — fans of Pugin will find much to interest them in it. See Joseph Aloysius Hansom (1803-82): An Unfortunate Genius.


    August 2026

    or many of us, this turned out to be another month of record-breaking heat. We have even found ourselves hankering after those rainy days at the seaside featured in Punch cartoons! Still, work continued here, and some brilliant new projects started.

    As usual, do check out the new conferences and calls for papers in our list. Several have deadlines coming up in the autumn. One that may tempt you is on the theme of design, for the 35th Annual 18th- and 19th-Century British Women Writers Conference: the scope here is huge, from the visual arts to literary forms to course and syllabus design. That last suggestion will be especially interesting for those of you looking forward to an academic career.

    As for new work on the website, thanks, first, to Judith Al-Seffar of the Pugin Society and Rev. Paul O'Connor of the Birmingham Diocesan Trust, for photographs of the Church of St Thomas and St Edmund, Erdington, designed by C.F. Hansom with much interior work by A.W.N. Pugin. A new entry on this was followed by second, this time on an elaborate altar and reredos by Hansom, and then an account of another of his churches in which Pugin did the internal furnishings, the Church of Our Lady and St Alphonsus, Hanley Swan. Many thanks again to Deacon O'Connor for his excellent photographs. It is high time we included C.F. Hansom, who is generally overshadowed by his more famous brother, J.A. Hansom; and the churches themselves, with their monastic connections, have fascinating histories. Inspired by these histories, JB opened a new section on monasticism in the nineteenth century, with an introduction to it (again with useful input from Deacon O'Connor).

    On topics that are part of this wider context, we have new book reviews that give insights into the intellectual ferment of the age. John P. Farrell's review of Writers and Revolution (2021), praises the way Jonathan Beecher explores and elucidates the complex responses of the most important French writers, including George Sand, Victor Hugo and Karl Marx, to the political upheaval on that side of the Channel. The review itself is usefully illuminating: Britain may have avoided the same kind of upheaval, but its reverberations were felt on this side of the Channel as well. Carol Senf's review of Janet Browne's Darwin brings home to us the extent to which Darwin too sent waves through the intellectual and spiritual life of the age. Browne's success in keeping the personality of the man himself in focus also becomes clear — did you know, for example, that Darwin was an animal-lover, and worried about the treatment of laboratory animals? Thanks as always to our Managing Editor Diane Josefowicz, for running our increasingly important and scholarly book review section.

    The first of this month's bigger projects to get underway is our Senior Editor's: Simon Cooke has been writing about the very special artwork in the Dalziels' Bible Gallery, with more than sixty plates by such major artists as Ford Madox Brown (for example, look at his dramatic Death of Eglon) and Frederick Leighton (whose Samson and the Lion really stops you in your tracks). Another of the illustrations, Haman Supplicating Esher, is by an artist who is new to us, E.F. Brewtnall, briefly introduced here. There's more to come for Brewtnall, and much more to come for the Dalziels' unique book.

    Simon has also been keeping up with the latest art exhibitions: the first to come in was his thought-provoking review of Richard Dadd – Beyond Bedlam at the Royal Academy, London, 25 July–25 October, 2026. This might encourage you to look at Dadd in rather a new light, or at least to recognise "the continuum between Dadd Not Mad and Mad Dadd." Simon then visited and reviewed James McNeill Whistler at Tate Britain (21 May–27 September 2026). Here too the emphasis is on seeing the oeuvre as a whole, tracing continuities, here especially the daring, controversial development towards Aestheticism and abstraction. Only one disappointment at the Tate — the absence of a key work, Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket. Otherwise, this is another five-star review, making a visit to the capital altogether irresistible.

    Another big project, still ongoing, is the revision of the work of Albert Moore in the painting section, as Dennis T. Lanigan adds commentaries, improved images, and many new entries to our large section of this artist's work. As a taster, take a peep at the new page on Moore's Azaleas. Moore has so much to recommend him (especially his love of flowers and music), and since Dennis admits to being a particular fan of his, this promises to be a rare treat.

    Back in the illustrations section, and involving new work on Edward Bulwer Lytton's aptly entitled A Strange Story, are some illustrations from a pirated edition of the novel, like this stagey one by F. O. Freeman, of a disappearing spook. Dealing with works like this, illustrated by different people, can be complicated, and A Strange Story was no exception. Philip has been helping, too, with updating and repairing links in older documents. All such help is greatly needed and very much appreciated.

    From Yifu Xing, a young scholar with a special interest in the emotive topic of child labour, comes a useful bibliography for the subject. Yifu has already visited the National Archives at Kew, and is currently preparing a much larger online resource for those researching the transformation of working-class childhood in Victorian times.

    Education was, of course, a vital aspect of this transformation. This month we have also expanded our section on pedagogy, with Philip Allingham pitching in here too to assemble at least a selection of our material on it. We hope that this will be useful to teachers and students alike. The new sitemap was inspired by an essay, "Why Jane Eyre?" by Derek Furr, a literature professor and Dean of Teacher Education at Bard College, examining the pros and cons of teaching a text "from the ascendency of the British empire, one with a rich critical heritage and the burden of canonicity." These are important and highly relevant considerations at a time when the humanities are under unprecedented pressure.

    Correspondence: Thank you to Euwyn at a Web Editions reader for pointing out that the whole text of Thomas Huxley's key essay, "Agnosticism," is available there in proofed and annotated form. A link has been added to the appropriate page, along with a link to the essay as it first appeared in the periodical Popular Science Monthly in 1889.

    In turn, we were pleased to hear that our pages have been useful to those at Senate House, London, as they prepare for this year's Open Day, London (yes, we do feel that this is a proper educational use of our photographs!); to Howard Leader, assistant editor of the Phoenix Newspaper in Birmingham, again for the use of architectural images; and to John Bedder-Green, of Scarborough Gallery, for permission to reproduce an image of Sir Joseph Noel Paton's Man of Sorrows for an information label or panel. We are not a free photo gallery, but where there are no copyright problems, the cause is good, and full credit is given, we are happy to help with such projects.

    Thank you also to Pamela Gerrish Nunn for updating her essay on Edith Martineau and Aestheticism in light of a recent re-attribution: Pamela was glad to be notified of this directly by one of our readers. Just a reminder here that Pamela is the curator of the upcoming exhibition of Pre-Raphaelitism Female Paths, 1850-1914 at the Recoletos Hall, the Mapfre Foundation, in Madrid (24 September to 10 January 2027). A city break for the autumn perhaps?



    July 2026

    uly opened quietly with long hot days; halfway through the month, the promise (or threat) of more to come has already been been amply fulfilled. Take a moment, if you can, to check out the latest conference listings: a new one on "Keywords in Religion and Victorian Studies" has a deadline for abstracts coming up on 25 July. But this may be the time to sit back and listen to our latest podcasts or take a look through some of our galleries. Here are a few suggestions: flower-patterned tiles designed by William De Morgan; furniture designed by Charles Rennie Mackintosh; and John Tenniel's illustrations for Alice in Wonderland. Click on the images to enlarge them and find out more about them.

    From the Pugin Society's publications this month, we reprint an article on A.W.N. Pugin's contributions to Gawthorpe Hall in Lancashire, written by the Site Supervisor, Rachel Pilling. The Hall's refurbishment by Charles Barry and Pugin would have been underway when Charlotte Brontë went to stay with the Kay-Shuttleworths there in March 1850: it is fascinating to think that she might have heard the two architects' work being discussed during her visit. She certainly appreciated the Hall's "picturesque" appearance. As for us, we appreciate the generosity of both the Society and Rachel herself for sharing this piece with us.

    As usual, we have several new projects in hand. JB offered an overview of the Victorians and Nature, drawing together ideas covered in many different areas of the website, from urban and suburban amenities to plein air painting and growing ecological concerns, as it became apparent that, to quote from William Morris's News from Nowhere, we could no longer see "everything, except mankind, animate and inanimate — “nature”, as people used to call it — as one thing, and mankind as another." In quite a different area, JB also added an account of the Indian architect, Ram Singh, and his work on the exotic Durbar Hall at Osborne.

    At the suggestion of our managing editor, Diane Josefowicz, JB brought in a little contemporary material on two influential Punch editors — Mark Lemon and Frank Burnand, so that they could be incorporated in our list of selected periodical editors and contributors. Who else should be there? Do let us know if you have any suggestions.

    In the painting section, Dennis T. Lanigan has now completed his thorough revision of the large section on the artist Sir Frank Dicksee, wherever possible replacing older monochrome images from magazines in the Internet Archive with colour ones now available from Art UK and elsewhere, and adding many more works, with useful commentaries. Here for example is the much-improved entry for one of his best-known works, Harmony, fourteen years after it was first put online, with the "glow of warm light" (as one art critic described it at the time) apparent at last. Having been subjected to Thomas Scott Buckle's critical eye, and enriched by some of his suggestions, this project is now complete. It does justice at last to one of the pre-eminent Pre-Raphaelites.

    Similarly, our contributing editor from Canada, Philip Allingham, has now completed his revision of John McLenan's illustrations for Edward Bulwer-Lytton's best seller, A Strange Story. If you don't know it you'll be able to follow along through the illustrations, each of which has now acquired a "passage illustrated" section and a commentary. Here for instance is the "enigmatic occultist Margrave" who apparently possesses the secret of eternal youth. Unfortunately, McLenan's illustrations for the serial in Harper's Weekly peter out before the ending, but Philip kindly provides the relevant grisly details from the text, at the end of the final illustration. You have been warned! Philip is now well into an overhaul of his section on Felix Octavius Darley, with helpful commentaries on illustrations like this one in Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities, "Ennobling rather than conveying accurately Dickens's image of the September Massacres" — again, despite Darley's angle on it, rather disturbing.

    On quite a different note, Tim Willasey-Wilsey sent in a particularly fine photograph of the carved overmantel of Bradford Town Hall's Banqueting Hall. This is an amazing piece of work, not easy to access but often commented on, and a testimony to the skills of the last partner in the celebrated Earp, Hobbs & Miller firm of architectural sculptors. Tim was there to give a talk at a literary festival, and we very much appreciate his thinking of us then.

    Indeed, we never know where new submissions will come from next: early this month we received a very interesting piece from history buff Vivekanand Singh, from Ballia, Uttar Pradesh, India, about the Telegraph Memorial in Delhi, alerting us to the historical background and role of the new telegraph system in the Sepoy Rebellion of 1857.

    This month, too, we are very pleased to offer a new full-length article, entitled "'You shall give me your signature to a little bill': Bills of Exchange and Promissory Notes in Victorian Fiction." This is by John Paschetto, a transactional lawyer with a particular interest in the history of bills and notes, and in how various novelists employed them in plot and character development. Giving insights into works as diverse as George Eliot's and Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's, and (for example) correcting a misreading of a passage in A Christmas Carol, this unusual approach turns out to be truly rewarding — at least for the readers!

    Correspondence: We were delighted to hear from one of our contributors, Penelope Harris, that her new book on the architect (and inventor of a certain kind of conveyance) Joseph Hansom is coming out in the autumn from Unicorn: check out Joseph Aloysius Hansom (1803-82): An Unfortunate Genius. Keith Wood writes to report the latest additions to his ever-expanding Great Exhibition simulation project, so you might also like to take a look now at Prince Albert's model dwellings, displayed just outside the Crystal Palace — all the more impressive when you know that these were later moved to Kennington and can still be seen there, albeit with some modifications.

    Special thanks to practising architect John Bowmer, who wrote in to correct some confusion of architectural features on blocks of flats opposite each other near the Royal Albert Hall (Albert Hall Mansions and Albert Court). We greatly appreciate such input. Thanks also to Bernard Richards, Emeritus Fellow of Brasenose College, Oxford, and an old friend of our founding Editor-in-Chief, George Landow, for sending in an appreciative comment on our "enormously useful" site. That makes all our work worthwhile!
